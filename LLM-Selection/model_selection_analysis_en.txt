PILOT BASE-MODEL SELECTION EXPERIMENT

1. Objective

This pilot experiment compared three instruction-tuned models in the approximately
three-billion-parameter deployment class for the constrained threat-identification
task used by the proposed system. The purpose was not to compare general language
model capability. Instead, the experiment examined whether each model could assess
a deterministic OWASP candidate against questionnaire evidence and a supplied data-
flow diagram while obeying a fixed structured-output contract.

The evaluated models were:

- qwen2.5:3b-instruct-q4_K_M
- llama3.2:3b-instruct-q4_K_M
- ministral-3:3b-instruct-2512-q4_K_M


2. Experimental Setup

The experiment was executed on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU through Google Colab. Ollama
0.34.0 was used as the inference runtime. All models used Q4_K_M quantization, an
8,192-token context window, temperature 0, and the same prompt and JSON schema.

The pilot set contained 15 controlled cases covering five OWASP risk families:

- LLM01: Prompt Injection
- LLM02: Sensitive Information Disclosure
- LLM05: Improper Output Handling
- LLM06: Excessive Agency
- API7:2023: Server-Side Request Forgery

Each risk family was represented by three counterfactual variants: a positive case
with explicit evidence of a control gap, a control case containing protective
controls, and an unknown case in which decisive security information was missing.
Every case was executed three times, resulting in 45 attempted calls per model and
135 attempted calls in total.

All usable responses conformed to the expected JSON shape, and all referenced DFD
identifiers were valid. However, Qwen completed only 30 of its 45 calls. Its other
15 calls reached the configured 300-second request timeout. The conditional status
score of 60.00% shown by the original benchmark summary therefore overstates Qwen's
operational performance. When the failed calls are retained in the denominator,
Qwen's effective status agreement is 40.00%, or 18 correct decisions from 45
attempts.

The 15 Qwen timeouts occurred in the following cases:

- TC-API7-POS-01: three timeouts
- TC-LLM01-CTRL-01: one timeout
- TC-LLM02-CTRL-01: three timeouts
- TC-LLM02-POS-01: three timeouts
- TC-LLM05-POS-01: three timeouts
- TC-LLM06-CTRL-01: one timeout
- TC-LLM06-POS-01: one timeout

At a 300-second timeout per failed request, these failures added approximately 75
minutes of waiting time. If each timeout is counted as a 300-second operational
latency, Qwen's mean latency per attempted call becomes approximately 103.35 seconds,
compared with 3.77 seconds for Llama and 4.99 seconds for Ministral. Consequently,
Qwen's 4.568-second median for successful calls must not be interpreted as its full
end-to-end reliability or latency.


4. Behaviour by Scenario Type

Llama and Ministral both completed all 15 positive, 15 control, and 15 unknown
attempts. Each achieved 60.00% agreement on positive cases, but both performed poorly
on control and unknown cases. Llama achieved 20.00% agreement on both control and
unknown cases. Ministral achieved 26.67% agreement on control cases and 20.00% on
unknown cases.

Qwen completed only five of the 15 positive attempts and ten of the 15 control
attempts. It achieved three correct positive decisions and no correct control
decisions when failures were retained in the denominator. In contrast, Qwen
correctly classified all 15 unknown attempts as needs_more_info. This indicates a
strong conservative bias: among its 30 successful responses, Qwen produced 27
needs_more_info decisions and three confirmed decisions, but no plausible or
not_applicable decisions.

Llama exhibited the opposite bias. Across all 45 calls, it produced only confirmed
or plausible decisions: 12 confirmed and 33 plausible. It never emitted
needs_more_info or not_applicable, even for explicitly unknown or strongly controlled
cases. Ministral produced 11 confirmed, 24 plausible, and 10 not_applicable decisions,
but never used needs_more_info. Therefore, none of the three models demonstrated
balanced use of all four status categories in this pilot.


5. Grounding, Pattern Selection, and Actionability

Ministral achieved the highest mean evidence coverage at 74.44%, followed by Llama
at 68.89%. Qwen's evidence coverage was considerably lower at 36.67% among its
successful responses. Qwen therefore made more expected status decisions than the
other models when it completed a request, but it cited substantially fewer expected
questionnaire evidence items.

Pattern selection was weak for all models. Llama selected
excessive_tool_or_workflow_agency in all 45 cases, irrespective of the target risk.
Qwen selected the same pattern in 27 of its 30 successful responses. Ministral used a
more varied set of patterns, but its exact pattern agreement was still only 13.33%.
This systematic behaviour suggests that pattern-selection accuracy should be
investigated separately and that the current prompt may not elicit reliable pattern
discrimination from models of this size.

Llama achieved 100.00% actionability conformance: it consistently populated an abuse
path and control gap for positive statuses and left them empty for non-positive
statuses. Qwen achieved 56.67% actionability conformance among successful calls.
Ministral achieved only 15.56%, despite its relatively high evidence coverage. Thus,
Ministral frequently cited relevant inputs without satisfying the required output
semantics.


6. Comparative Interpretation

Qwen produced the highest conditional status agreement and the highest output-token
rate. It also handled unknown cases consistently. Nevertheless, a 66.67% completion
rate is below a reasonable reliability threshold for a pipeline component. Its
timeouts, low evidence coverage, absence of not_applicable decisions, and narrow
pattern selection prevent it from being declared the winner of the current run.

Ministral was fully reliable at the transport and parsing level and achieved the
highest evidence coverage. Its status agreement was slightly higher than Llama's,
but it was the slowest model and had very low actionability conformance. It also
failed to represent uncertainty through needs_more_info.

Llama was the fastest model, completed all calls, maintained valid grounding, and
achieved perfect actionability conformance. Its principal weakness was semantic
discrimination: it overused plausible and collapsed every threat-pattern decision to
the same pattern. Its status agreement was 2.23 percentage points lower than
Ministral's, but this small difference was accompanied by a much stronger operational
profile.


7. Provisional Selection Decision

Applying the precondition that a candidate model must first complete requests
reliably, Qwen does not pass the reliability gate in this run. Between the two models
with 100% completion, Llama 3.2 3B provides the strongest operational baseline because
it has the lowest median and P95 latency and the highest actionability conformance.
Accordingly, if a model had to be selected solely from this run, Llama 3.2 3B would be
the provisional operational choice.

This is not yet a final scientific base-model selection. The result is based on only
15 unique cases from five risk families, and the three repeated executions are not
45 independent scenarios. In addition, all three models showed systematic status or
pattern biases. The experiment should therefore be described as a pilot study used
to refine the benchmark rather than conclusive evidence of general threat-
identification quality.


8. Required Benchmark Revision

Before the final comparison, the benchmark should be revised as follows:

1. Add a fixed output-token limit for every model. A limit such as 512 generated
   tokens is sufficient for a single-candidate case and prevents unbounded generation
   from consuming the entire 300-second timeout.
2. Count timeouts and invalid outputs as failures in every primary metric rather than
   excluding them from the denominator.
3. Report a status confusion matrix and macro-averaged F1 in addition to simple
   status agreement.
4. Preserve completion rate, evidence coverage, DFD grounding, actionability, median
   latency, P95 latency, and token throughput as separate metrics.
5. Examine whether threat_pattern should remain a predicted field or be supplied as
   a deterministic investigation lens, because two models collapsed heavily toward
   a single pattern.
6. Repeat the corrected 15-case pilot before extending the benchmark to all 29 OWASP
   risk families.

The corrected pilot will determine whether Qwen's timeout behaviour is caused by
uncapped generation or represents a persistent model-runtime compatibility problem.
Only after that rerun should the selected model be used as the base checkpoint for
the LoRA fine-tuning comparison.
